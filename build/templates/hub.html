How to read these pages

Maths is not read in a straight line, so the pages are built for jumping around. Every mention of a numbered result, like Theorem 1.11, is a link. Hover over it to read the statement where you are, click to go there, and use the ↩ back button that appears bottom-left to return. The small ⇥ on a hover card opens the result in a panel beside the text instead, which is handy when a proof keeps leaning on it. On a phone, tap once to preview and again to go.

The contents of the chapter you are in sit on the left, with the section you are reading marked, and a thin bar shows how far through you are. Under each section heading is a rough reading time, what the section contains, and two small gauges: how hard it is, and how much the rest of the course relies on it. They are guesses, made by someone who has read ahead.

The small labels in the left margin say what a stretch of text is for: the question sets up a problem, intuition gives the picture to keep in mind, motivation explains why the next definition is needed, in words restates a result, technique marks a trick that will come back, careful flags a pitfall, and connection links to another part of the course. A thin line beside the text shows how far each label reaches. On the right, a miniature of the whole chapter shows where you are; drag the outlined box, or click anywhere on it, to move.

Numbering follows the lecture notes, and the small page numbers in the margin point into them. Grey Background boxes explain material the notes take from elsewhere. Exercise boxes show only a name until you open them, and the answer stays folded until you ask for it. When enough has been covered for a problem-sheet question, a pencilled note says so.

Select any passage, formula included, to leave yourself a note on it. Notes are private: in the published version they are stored against your account and visible only to you; in a local copy they live in this browser. The notes button in the top bar lists them and exports them as Markdown.
